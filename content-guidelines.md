# Global UX Research Portfolio - Content Guidelines

## Voice & Tone Framework

### Overall Voice Characteristics

**Confident** → Display mastery and expertise without arrogance
- Example: "I specialize in strategic user research" (not "I'm an expert at everything")
- Avoid: False modesty, hedging language, excessive disclaimers

**Concise** → Communicate clearly in minimal words
- Example: "Influenced product roadmap through user research" (not "Through conducting comprehensive user research activities, I was able to provide insights that influenced...")
- Avoid: Fluff, unnecessary jargon, over-explanation

**Authentic** → Feel genuine and human
- Example: "This project taught me that research alone doesn't drive change—you need stakeholder buy-in" (not generic corporate language)
- Avoid: Corporate clichés, overly polished language that obscures meaning

**Accessible** → Written for diverse audiences, including those unfamiliar with UX research
- Example: "User interviews are conversations with real people who use the product" (not assuming jargon knowledge)
- Avoid: Unexplained acronyms, assumed domain knowledge, academic language

## Writing Principles

### 1. Focus on Outcomes, Not Activities

**Outcome-focused:**
- "Research showed users abandoned checkout when faced with 5+ form fields, so we simplified to 3 fields. Conversion improved 23%."

**Activity-focused (avoid):**
- "Conducted 12 user interviews, analyzed the data, and created a research report."

**Why:** Hiring managers care about *what changed*, not *what you did*.

---

### 2. Show Impact, Not Process

**Impact-focused:**
- "This finding led to a redesigned onboarding flow that reduced support tickets by 30%."

**Process-focused (avoid):**
- "We conducted thematic analysis on the interview transcripts using a affinity mapping approach."

**Why:** Impact proves value. Process is secondary.

---

### 3. Lead with Numbers

**Numbers first:**
- "24% of users couldn't find their saved items. Solution: visibility improvements reduced the gap to 3%."

**Numbers second (avoid):**
- "Users struggled to find their saved items. We discovered that 24% couldn't locate them."

**Why:** Numbers grab attention and quantify impact immediately.

---

### 4. Be Specific, Not Generic

**Specific:**
- "E-commerce users aged 35-55 abandoned checkout after seeing shipping costs; younger users (18-25) proceeded despite cost."

**Generic (avoid):**
- "Different user segments had different needs."

**Why:** Specificity demonstrates depth of insight.

---

### 5. Show Strategic Thinking

**Strategic:**
- "Rather than redesigning the interface, research revealed the core issue was information architecture. We restructured the navigation, which proved more effective than visual changes."

**Tactical (avoid):**
- "We made several design changes based on user feedback."

**Why:** Shows decision-making capability beyond execution.

---

### 6. Highlight Collaboration

**Collaborative:**
- "Worked with the product and design teams to translate research into a revised roadmap. Advocacy from 2 key stakeholders was critical to adoption."

**Solo-focused (avoid):**
- "I identified the key insights and recommended changes."

**Why:** Demonstrates ability to work across teams and influence others.

---

### 7. Acknowledge Constraints & Tradeoffs

**Realistic:**
- "Timeline constraints meant we couldn't conduct longitudinal research, so we used diary studies as an alternative. Trade-off: less depth, but quicker insights."

**Avoiding constraints (avoid):**
- "We conducted comprehensive research without limitations."

**Why:** Shows realistic project understanding and problem-solving.

## Tone by Section

### Homepage

**Tone:** Confident, welcoming, clear

**Characteristics:**
- Direct and punchy
- Action-oriented language
- Benefit-focused
- Professional but approachable

**Example Headlines:**
- "Strategic User Research That Shapes Product Decisions"
- "Translating User Needs Into Product Impact"
- "Research-Driven Design for 15+ Million Users"

**Example Copy:**
- "I help product teams understand user needs deeply—and translate those insights into measurable business outcomes."
- "My research influences product strategy, reduces support costs, and increases user satisfaction."

**Avoid:**
- Generic phrases like "passionate about research"
- Unclear positioning
- Overly academic language
- Weak CTAs

---

### Case Studies

**Tone:** Narrative, insightful, outcome-focused

**Characteristics:**
- Storytelling approach
- Balance of personal perspective and objectivity
- Emphasis on decision-making
- Specific metrics and outcomes
- Honest about challenges and learnings

**Example Opening:**
- "The team believed the issue was navigation complexity. Research revealed the real problem was a mismatch between user mental models and information architecture."

**Example Insights Section:**
- "The most surprising finding wasn't about the interface—it was about trust. Users would tolerate friction if they understood why it existed."

**Example Reflection:**
- "This project taught me that good research doesn't guarantee adoption. I learned to front-load stakeholder alignment and focus on translating findings into business language."

**Content Structure Pattern:**
1. Hook with the core insight or challenge
2. Context and objectives
3. Methodology with reasoning
4. Key findings with specificity
5. Strategic recommendations
6. Impact and outcomes
7. Personal reflection and learning

**Avoid:**
- Focusing on research methods over findings
- Generic "user said X" quotes without context
- Hiding impact or metrics
- Unclear recommendations
- Process-heavy explanations
- Jargon without explanation

### About

**Tone:** Personal, authentic, professional

**Characteristics:**
- Humanizing and relatable
- Storytelling approach
- Values-driven
- Balance of professional and personal
- Conversational but polished

**Example Personal Story Angle:**
- "I started as a designer thinking I knew what users wanted. Three years into user research, I realized I knew almost nothing. That humility shaped how I approach research today."

**Example Values Statement:**
- "I believe that accessibility isn't compliance—it's fundamental to good research. Every study I run ensures we're hearing from diverse users, not just the easy-to-reach ones."

**Example Interest Sharing:**
- "When I'm not conducting research, you'll find me exploring Lagos's food scene, contributing to open-source design projects, or helping junior researchers develop their craft."

**Content Approach:**
- Tell a story about how you became interested in research
- Explain what drives your work
- Share values that guide your approach
- Make it personal but professional
- Show personality without oversharing

**Avoid:**
- Generic "I'm passionate about helping users" statements
- Over-sharing personal details unrelated to work
- Corporate-sounding language
- Humble-bragging
- Lists without context (e.g., just listing companies without explaining impact)

### Research Process

**Tone:** Educational, transparent, methodical

**Characteristics:**
- Clear and explanatory (for readers unfamiliar with research)
- Confident in methodology
- Balance of philosophy and practice
- Open about limitations and tradeoffs
- Outcome-focused

**Example Philosophy Section:**
- "Great research starts with clarity. Before designing a study, I spend time aligning stakeholders on what questions actually matter and what decisions the research will inform. Misaligned research, no matter how rigorous, won't drive action."

**Example Method Description:**
- "User interviews are my go-to method for understanding the 'why' behind behavior. I conduct 8-15 interviews per study to identify patterns while maintaining the depth that only conversation allows. For breadth, I combine interviews with surveys or analytics analysis."

**Example Accessibility Commitment:**
- "Inclusive research isn't an afterthought. It's built into recruitment, data collection, and analysis. I ensure our participant pool reflects diverse demographics, abilities, and contexts. Research that only hears from a narrow slice of users isn't actually research—it's confirmation bias."

**Example Tradeoff Transparency:**
- "Quantitative studies provide statistical confidence but lose the nuance of why. Qualitative research goes deep but lacks statistical power. The best approach usually combines both, acknowledging what each trade-off gains and loses."

**Content Approach:**
- Explain each method in plain language
- Show when and why to use each method
- Acknowledge strengths and limitations
- Connect process to outcomes
- Demonstrate accessibility and inclusive thinking
- Make it educational for people new to research

**Avoid:**
- Overly academic explanations
- Unexplained jargon
- Positioning one method as universally best
- Hiding limitations
- Focusing on rigor over outcomes

### Community & Speaking

**Tone:** Proud, inclusive, forward-looking

**Characteristics:**
- Celebratory (of contributions and impact)
- Generous (sharing knowledge)
- Community-oriented
- Concrete impact metrics

**Example Speaking Entry:**
- "Building Design Thinking into Enterprise Research" at [Conference Name], 2024. 250+ attendees. Explored how to apply human-centered design thinking in large organizations where research budgets and stakeholder alignment are complex."

**Example Open-Source Entry:**
- "Contributed accessibility audit process to [Project], adopted by 12+ companies. Created documentation and training materials that reduced audit time by 40%."

**Example Mentorship Entry:**
- "Mentor 3 junior researchers entering the field. Focus on research planning, stakeholder management, and translating findings into organizational action. Proud to see mentees advance to senior research roles."

**Content Approach:**
- Lead with impact (not just participation)
- Show breadth of contributions
- Connect to larger community benefit
- Include outcomes where measurable
- Celebrate others' work alongside your own

**Avoid:**
- Just listing participation without impact
- Self-aggrandizing language
- Focusing only on your achievements (ignore broader impact)
- Vague descriptions ("gave a talk")

## Common Content Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Focusing on What You Did, Not What Changed

**Mistake:**
"Conducted 15 user interviews and created affinity maps."

**Better:**
"Interviews revealed that users couldn't find their saved items. We restructured the navigation, reducing support tickets by 28%."

---

### 2. Using Passive Voice or Vague Attribution

**Mistake:**
"The findings informed the product direction."

**Better:**
"The research showed that X, which led the product team to deprioritize feature Y and focus on Z instead."

---

### 3. Hiding Behind Jargon

**Mistake:**
"Conducted thematic analysis using a mixed-methods approach with triangulation of multiple data sources."

**Better:**
"Analyzed interviews and usage data together to identify patterns. This combination showed us where user intentions and actual behavior diverged."

---

### 4. Forgetting to Mention Constraints

**Mistake:**
"Conducted comprehensive research."

**Better:**
"With a 6-week timeline, we prioritized qualitative interviews over longitudinal studies, accepting less depth but gaining speed to decision."

---

### 5. Making Generic Statements

**Mistake:**
"Users had different needs and preferences."

**Better:**
"Power users completed tasks 3x faster but required advanced options. New users needed guided workflows. This segmentation drove our two-tier design approach."

---

### 6. Losing the Narrative

**Mistake:**
Listing methods, findings, and recommendations as separate sections without connecting them.

**Better:**
Tell a story: "We suspected X. Interviews confirmed Y. This led to decision Z. The outcome was W."

---

### 7. Underselling Influence

**Mistake:**
"Presented recommendations to the team."

**Better:**
"The research directly contradicted the team's initial hypothesis. Getting buy-in required reframing the findings in business terms. These conversations led to a product shift that improved retention by 15%."

## Case Study Template - Copy Framework

### [Project Title]

**[1-2 sentence headline/hook that captures the core insight or challenge]**

---

## Context

[2-3 sentences establishing business/product context. What industry? What was the company doing? What was the market environment?]

---

## The Challenge

[1-2 sentences stating what the team was trying to figure out or what problem they were trying to solve.]

---

## Research Objectives

[2-3 bullet points. What specific questions needed answering? What decisions would this research inform?]

---

## My Role & Approach

[2-3 sentences. What was your specific responsibility? How did you approach the problem? Who were your collaborators?]

---

## What We Did

[4-6 sentences describing the research activities. Who did you talk to? What methods did you use? How many participants? Timeline? Be specific and explain *why* you chose this approach.]

---

## Key Findings

[6-10 specific findings. Use numbers where possible. Start with most surprising or impactful. Example: "X% of users did Y, revealing Z insight."]

---

## What It Meant

[2-4 sentences interpreting the findings. What did this mean for the product? For the business? What was the strategic implication?]

---

## What Changed

[2-3 sentences. What specific decisions did the research influence? Which features were built, deprioritized, or redesigned as a result?]

---

## The Outcome

[Specific metrics and impact. Examples: "Conversion improved from X% to Y%", "Support tickets reduced by X%", "Retention increased by X%", "User satisfaction moved from X to Y."]

---

## What I Learned

[1-2 sentences reflecting on the project. What did it teach you? What would you do differently? How did it shape your future approach?]

## Writing Audit Checklist

Use this for reviewing all portfolio content:

**Clarity**
- [ ] Can someone unfamiliar with UX research understand this?
- [ ] Are terms explained where necessary?
- [ ] Is the core message clear in the first sentence?

**Impact**
- [ ] Does it emphasize outcomes over process?
- [ ] Are metrics or specific impact included?
- [ ] Does it answer "so what?"

**Voice**
- [ ] Does it sound authentic?
- [ ] Is the tone appropriate for the section?
- [ ] Does it avoid corporate clichés?

**Accessibility**
- [ ] Is the sentence length reasonable (under 20 words where possible)?
- [ ] Are there short paragraphs with whitespace?
- [ ] Is complex information broken into digestible chunks?

**Engagement**
- [ ] Does it tell a story or narrative?
- [ ] Are there concrete examples?
- [ ] Would a reader want to learn more?

**Credibility**
- [ ] Is it specific, not generic?
- [ ] Are claims substantiated?
- [ ] Does it avoid over-claiming?
